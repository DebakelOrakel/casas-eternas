This note is a reference for the table "The earth's timeline" in
[DES-0032](./prehistory-in-three-steps.md). It does not change DES-0032.

Conventions:

- Year 0 is the start of farming in the Near East, the start of the PPNA,
  at **9 600 BC**. Offset = 9 600 − (date BC). A positive offset is after
  the start of farming.
- All dates are calibrated (cal BC). A date in cal BP is converted with
  BC = BP − 1 950; a date in b2k with BC = b2k − 2 000.
- "Verified" means the number was read in the paper, its abstract, or the
  excavation project's own page during this check. "Not verified" means the
  number comes from memory or from a secondary source only.

Notes on the rows:

- **Natufian.** The 15–11.5 ka cal BP range is from AMS dates at
  el-Wad Terrace and Raqefet Cave [1]. The Early/Late split has weak
  absolute support; the Early Natufian hamlets with stone houses
  (Ain Mallaha, el-Wad) are the "settled villages".
- **Göbekli Tepe.** The DAI team itself calls its dates preliminary.
  Organic material in the fill can be older or younger than the
  enclosures [3], [4].
- **Melos.** Franchthi is some 120 km from Melos in a direct line, with
  islets in between. A route through Attica needs crossings of only
  15–20 km [5].
- **Cyprus.** The distance (some 70 km to Anatolia, near 100 km to the
  Levant) is a map value, not a number from a cited paper. People
  visited Cyprus before farming (Akrotiri Aetokremnos, Late
  Epipalaeolithic); that date is not verified here.
- **Çatalhöyük.** Kuijt and Marciniak [12] assume 40 % of the mound
  built over, 70 % of houses in use, about five people per house. Their
  figure is contested (houses empty too often in their model). For a
  game number, "1 000 – 3 000 at the peak" is a fair middle; it is a
  judgement, not a source.
- **Tin bronze.** Radivojević et al. [30] state the standard account:
  the earliest regular tin bronzes of the Near East are early 3rd
  millennium BC.

**The fall-off with distance (Renfrew).** Renfrew, Dixon and Cann [33]
were the first to plot obsidian abundance against distance from the
source. Renfrew later named two zones [34]:

- **Supply zone**, up to ~300 km from the source: obsidian is more than
  80 % of the chipped stone.
- **Contact zone**, beyond ~300 km: the share falls off exponentially
  with distance (a straight line on a log scale).

The 300 km and 80 % values are from secondary sources; the original
text was not seen.

Ibáñez et al. [34] model the same data. Numbers from their paper:

- Southern Levant sites 700 – 800 km from the Anatolian sources have
  obsidian at about **0.5 %** of the flint.
- In a down-the-line model where each village keeps half and passes half
  on, the share is **0.39 % after eight steps**.
- To fit the data with that model, villages must be about **100 km**
  apart. Local links are 50 km.
- A pure down-the-line chain fits the PPNA with links up to 180 km. For
  the PPNB it needs links of 300 km. Ibáñez et al. conclude that
  long-distance links, not only neighbour-to-neighbour exchange, carried
  the obsidian.
- PPNB sites more than 500 km away: obsidian/flint ratio by site size,
  small 0.073 %, medium 0.398 %, big 2.41 %. Big sites get more: a sign
  of central places in the network.

Rule of thumb for a model: the share halves for each exchange step, and
large villages get several times more than small ones at the same
distance.

## The 8.2 ka event

- **Date.** Greenland ice cores: 8.30 – 8.14 ka b2k, which is
  **c. 6 300 – 6 140 BC**; offset **+3 300 … +3 460** [40].
- **Duration.** 160.5 ± 5.5 years in all. Inside it, a central event of
  69 ± 2 years (8.26 – 8.19 ka b2k, 6 260 – 6 190 BC) [40]. Most proxy
  records around the globe show 100 – 150 years [41].
- **Magnitude, Greenland.** Cooling of 3.3 ± 1.1 °C (decadal mean), in
  less than ~20 years [42].
- **Magnitude, Europe.** About −1 °C and about −20 % precipitation in
  parts of Europe [41].
- **Near East.** The pattern is cooler around the North Atlantic and
  drier in the northern tropics [41]. Direct evidence in the Near East is
  scarce. At Çatalhöyük, the hydrogen isotopes of animal fats in pots
  record the event [13]. A review of the radiocarbon dates of Southwest
  Asia from 9 500 to 7 500 cal BP finds **no simultaneous collapse, no
  large abandonment and no migration** at the 9.2 and 8.2 ka events,
  only local adaptation [43]. A quantitative drop in Near East rainfall
  was not found in this check.
- **Cause.** The drainage of glacial Lake Agassiz into the North
  Atlantic is the usual cause. Not verified here.

For the game: a short (one to two lifetimes) cold, dry shock that
farmers survive by local change, not a reset.

## The end of the African Humid Period

- **Span.** Off Mauritania (ODP 658C, ~20° N), the humid period lasts
  from 14.8 to **5.5 ka BP (c. 3 550 BC, offset +6 050)**. Both its start
  and its end are abrupt, within decades to centuries [44].
- **Abrupt or gradual.** It depends on the place.
  - Lake Yoa (northern Chad): **gradual** drying of the land over
    6 000 years; only the lake itself changes abruptly, at its own
    thresholds. Conditions like today's from c. 2 700 years ago [45].
  - A synthesis of records across Africa: the end is **locally abrupt
    but later at lower latitudes**, near 5 ka BP at 20° N and near 3 ka
    BP at 6.5° N (Lake Bosumtwi) [46].
- **People and the Nile.** Radiocarbon data from some 150 excavations in
  the Eastern Sahara: settlement starts with the humid conditions at
  c. 8 500 BC; **gradual drying from c. 5 300 BC (offset +4 300)** moves
  people to the Nile valley and to the south. The authors see this as a
  motor of the rise of pharaonic Egypt [47].

For the game: the Sahara empties over some 2 000 years, from north to
south, and the river valley gains people. It is not a single year.
